# AAISE Current Problem State v1

## Purpose

This document defines the current problem state for the proposed AAISE
(Associates AI Software Engineering) degree effort.

It is not a solution document.

It is meant to clarify what has changed, why the current program structure is
no longer sufficient on its own, and why a staged transition approach may be
necessary.

---

## What Has Changed

Wisconsin Technical College software-related programs were largely designed in
a pre-AI context.

That does not mean they are now obsolete. It does mean they were not built for
a labor market in which AI is increasingly present across software work,
product workflows, business processes, and application architecture.

This shift is already recognized across the WTCS environment, which is one of
the reasons the May 11 monthly IT programs meeting is focused on the need to
refactor software-related degrees and programs in response to AI.

The question is no longer whether change is needed.

The more important question is what kind of change is actually required.

---

## The Core Problem

The problem is not simply that current software programs are missing AI topics.

The deeper problem is that current programs were designed for an earlier model
of software practice, while students are now entering a workforce that is
operating across multiple development modes at the same time.

If curriculum change is framed only as "adding AI tools" or "teaching prompt
use," the redesign will likely undershoot the actual shift taking place in the
field.

The real challenge is to prepare students for a mixed development environment
in which conventional software engineering still matters, but is increasingly
interacting with AI-assisted workflows, AI-enabled applications, and internal
AI-driven business systems.

---

## The New Workforce Reality

Students will likely need to be prepared to work across at least four
overlapping models of practice.

### 1. Native Development

This remains conventional software development without meaningful AI
integration.

Students still need strong foundations here because many employers, systems,
and codebases will continue to operate in largely non-AI environments for some
time.

### 2. AI-Assisted Development

This includes the growing use of AI tools to support coding, debugging,
drafting, testing, documentation, and related development tasks.

This is often the first form of AI adoption organizations encounter. It is also
the form most likely to be misunderstood as the entire AI shift.

### 3. AI-Injected Development

This includes conventional applications and systems that are being updated to
include AI-supported capabilities, workflows, or user experiences.

This is not just tool use by the developer. It is the design and construction
of software that incorporates AI in a governed, intentional way.

### 4. AI-Embedded Systems

This includes applications, workflows, or business systems in which AI becomes
part of the internal operational logic of the system itself, including
descriptive, predictive, and generative or transformative use on business data.

If needed, this term can be clarified further in later artifacts, but it is
intended here as broader than narrow ML engineering alone.

---

## Why the Current Curriculum Is Insufficient

The current IT Software Developer program, like most existing technical college
software programs, is essentially sans AI at every level.

That means the issue is not limited to one missing course, one missing topic,
or one outdated description.

It means the current structure was not originally designed to develop students
for the mixed-mode environment now emerging in industry.

This does not invalidate the existing program. It does, however, suggest that
incremental updates alone may not be enough unless those updates are guided by
a larger transition logic.

---

## The Transitional Constraint

A new AI-native program, if developed, will not instantly replace the current
program in practice.

There will likely be an overlap period of at least one year in which:

- some students are finishing the current program
- some students may enter a revised bridge version
- some students may later transition into a new program structure
- faculty and institutions will be adapting in stages

This makes transition design a central requirement, not a side issue.

The easier it is for students to move between the current and future structures,
or to complete one while others begin the next, the more coherent and workable
the implementation will be.

---

## The Immediate Local Reality at Southwest Tech

Southwest Tech has already begun a first attempt at AI injection within the
current approved software program structure.

At present, that work has only been applied to the first semester of the full
four-semester program, and it has not yet been taught.

That means this effort should be treated honestly as an early design-stage
prototype, not as a validated finished model.

This is not a weakness. It is simply part of the current reality.

In fact, that early prototype may still be highly valuable as a source of
insight for identifying what is easy, what is difficult, what does not map
cleanly, and what must eventually change more substantially in a future AAISE
program.

---

## The Emerging Design Need

Given the current state, the design challenge appears to have two connected
tracks.

### Track 1. AI-Injected Revision of the Current Program

The current approved IT Software Developer program can serve as the starting
point for a staged AI-injected redesign.

This track supports near-term action within existing governance constraints and
creates a more realistic bridge for students, faculty, and institutional
implementation.

### Track 2. AAISE Successor Program Design

At the same time, the state of the field suggests the need for a more coherent
AI-era degree model that is not merely the old program plus added AI content.

This future program would need to be designed more intentionally around the
realities of native, AI-assisted, AI-injected, and AI-embedded software
practice.

These two tracks should not be treated as separate unrelated efforts.

The first can inform the second, and the second can help guide how the first is
refactored.

---

## Why Problem Framing Matters First

Before drafting a white paper or proposing a final curriculum model, the
problem state must be defined clearly.

If the problem is framed too narrowly, the resulting redesign may reduce AI to
tool familiarity.

If the problem is framed too abstractly, the resulting proposal may become hard
to implement within current institutional constraints.

The goal is to define the problem at the right level:

- broad enough to reflect the actual shift in software education
- concrete enough to support action
- honest enough to acknowledge present limits
- structured enough to guide both transitional and future program design

---

## Working Problem Statement

The current WTCS software curriculum structure was designed for a pre-AI
development environment and is therefore insufficient, in its present form, to
prepare students for the emerging mixed-mode software workforce.

The issue is not merely the absence of AI topics. The deeper challenge is that
students now need preparation across multiple overlapping modes of practice:
native development, AI-assisted development, AI-injected development, and
AI-embedded systems.

Because these modes coexist in industry, a modern degree model must prepare
students to work across them rather than treat AI as a narrow tool add-on.

At the same time, approval constraints and overlapping implementation timelines
mean that any transition must support both near-term revision of the current
program and longer-term development of a more coherent AI-era successor.

The central design problem is therefore not only curricular modernization, but
the creation of a workable transition architecture that preserves employability,
supports student mobility, and enables coherent institutional adoption.

---

## Next Questions

The next design-stage questions likely include:

- What outcomes should graduates of the future program actually demonstrate?
- Which parts of the current program can be revised through AI injection, and
  which parts point toward a different program structure altogether?
- What transition logic would allow students to move between old and new
  program pathways with minimal disruption?
- How should this be presented in a way that is persuasive at both the local
  and statewide level?

# AAISE Constraints and Design Realities v1

## Purpose

This document identifies the major constraints and operating realities shaping
the design of a proposed AAISE (Associates AI Software Engineering) degree and
the transition from the current IT Software Developer program.

It is not a complaint document.

Its purpose is to make constraints explicit early so that later design work is
more coherent, more honest, and more implementable.

---

## Why This Artifact Matters

Curriculum redesign efforts often become less effective when hidden constraints
are discovered too late.

In this case, the challenge is not simply to imagine an ideal future program.
It is to design a viable path from the current state to a stronger future
state, while working inside governance, timing, staffing, and delivery
realities that cannot simply be ignored.

Some constraints may eventually soften. Others may remain fixed for some time.

Both kinds still need to be recognized.

---

## Constraint Category 1. Governance and Approval Reality

The current software-related programs exist within a statewide technical
college governance structure.

That means program-level change does not happen in a vacuum. Even when local
innovation is possible, it still exists within broader approval processes,
advisory expectations, and system-level coordination.

At present, proposed program changes must be submitted to the advisory
committee for review and approval by August 1. Once approved, changes would
then be implemented in the curriculum for the next academic year.

This creates a real design condition:

- ideas must be persuasive enough for review
- proposals must be structured enough to evaluate
- timing matters
- local experimentation must still be legible within statewide process

This also means that a concept can be directionally correct, yet still fail if
it is not framed in a governance-friendly way.

---

## Constraint Category 2. Transitional Rather Than Greenfield Design

This effort does not begin from a blank slate.

Southwest Tech is currently working within the approved IT Software Developer
program structure rather than launching an entirely unconstrained new program
immediately.

That means the problem is not only how to invent a better future degree. It is
also how to work intelligently with what already exists.

This creates several implications:

- some existing program elements may need to be preserved temporarily
- some course descriptions may be changed only modestly
- content changes may be more flexible than structural changes
- near-term redesign may need to function as a bridge rather than an endpoint

This favors a staged design approach over a one-step replacement mindset.

---

## Constraint Category 3. Overlap Between Legacy and Future Programs

If a future AAISE program is approved, the current and future program
structures will likely overlap for at least one year.

This is not a minor administrative detail. It is a real design condition.

During that overlap period:

- some students may be completing the current program
- some may begin in a revised bridge version
- some may want or need to transition into the new structure
- advising, scheduling, and equivalency decisions will matter

Because of this, the design must support transition compatibility.

Students should not become stranded between old and new structures simply
because the redesign logic was academically strong but operationally brittle.

---

## Constraint Category 4. Uneven Current State of AI Injection

At present, Southwest Tech's AI-injection work appears to be an early-stage
prototype rather than a finished redesign.

AI-oriented revision has only been applied to the first semester of the
four-semester program, and that revision has not yet been taught.

This creates both a limitation and an opportunity.

The limitation is that the current work should not be overstated as validated
proof of a complete model.

The opportunity is that this early work can still provide real insight into:

- what maps cleanly onto the current structure
- what becomes overloaded
- what assumptions fail under closer inspection
- what changes may require a different long-term program architecture

This means the current prototype should be treated as design evidence, but not
yet as curriculum proof.

---

## Constraint Category 5. Mixed-State Industry Reality

Industry is not moving in a single clean direction.

Some employers remain largely conventional in their software practices. Others
are adopting AI primarily as a productivity tool. Others are beginning to
inject AI into applications and workflows. Still others are embedding AI into
business systems more directly.

Because of this, the target environment for graduates is mixed rather than
uniform.

This creates a major curriculum constraint:

the program cannot be optimized only for a fully AI-native future while
ignoring the continued reality of conventional and partially transitioned
environments.

At the same time, the program also cannot remain anchored only to conventional
practice and assume that AI capability can be added later as a minor layer.

The curriculum must prepare students for coexistence across multiple operating
models.

---

## Constraint Category 6. Risk of Shallow AI Framing

One of the most likely distortions in redesign work is reducing AI preparation
to tool familiarity alone.

If the redesign is framed too narrowly, AI may be interpreted as:

- prompt use
- chatbot familiarity
- code-assistant exposure
- optional productivity enhancement

Those elements may matter, but they are not sufficient on their own.

The curriculum problem is broader. Students will need to understand not only
how to use AI tools, but also how AI changes workflows, architecture,
evaluation, reliability, system boundaries, and professional judgment.

This is an interpretive constraint as much as a curricular one.

The language used in planning artifacts will strongly influence whether the
resulting program becomes transformational or superficial.

---

## Constraint Category 7. Need to Preserve Employability Fundamentals

Even with substantial AI change, students will still need strong software
foundations.

That includes areas such as:

- problem framing
- logic and algorithms
- software design
- debugging and testing
- data handling
- integration thinking
- communication and documentation

If AI is introduced in a way that weakens these foundations, the redesign may
produce graduates who appear current but are actually more fragile in practice.

This means the curriculum must modernize without abandoning the durable
foundations of software work.

The goal is not replacement of fundamentals, but reframing and extension of
them.

---

## Constraint Category 8. Faculty and Delivery Reality

Any program redesign must eventually be teachable, supportable, and repeatable.

That means the design cannot assume unlimited faculty time, instant faculty
retraining, or frictionless institutional adaptation.

Even a strong new model may fail if:

- its delivery demands are too abrupt
- its expectations are too abstract
- its materials are too immature
- its implementation path is too dependent on one person's informal knowledge

This does not argue against ambitious change.

It argues for designing ambition in a way that can survive contact with actual
delivery conditions.

---

## Constraint Category 9. Need for Statewide Legibility

If Southwest Tech hopes to influence broader WTCS redesign, the resulting logic
must be understandable beyond the local context.

That means the model should not depend too heavily on:

- local shorthand
- highly idiosyncratic terminology
- assumptions shared only by a small design group

This does not mean avoiding new concepts.

It does mean that concepts must be introduced in a way that others can inspect,
translate, and adapt.

This is especially important for frameworks such as RBA, where the underlying
design logic may be highly valuable even if the terminology itself is still new.

---

## Constraint Category 10. Time and Sequencing Reality

There is limited time between the current framing stage, the May 11 meeting,
and the August 1 advisory review timeline.

This means sequencing matters.

Not everything has to be finished at once, but the right things do need to be
clear in the right order.

At this stage, what matters most is likely:

- correct problem framing
- clear articulation of the mixed workforce reality
- a defensible transition logic
- a credible path for local pilot development

What likely does not need to be fully completed yet:

- a fully finalized new program architecture
- a perfectly finished four-semester AI-injected redesign
- total consensus on all terminology

This suggests the current task is not full completion. It is structured
advancement with enough coherence to guide the next stage.

---

## Design Implications

Taken together, these constraints suggest several early design implications.

### 1. A dual-track approach is likely necessary

The current program likely needs an AI-injected bridge path, while a future
AAISE structure is developed more intentionally as a successor.

### 2. Transition compatibility must be designed on purpose

Student mobility, equivalency, and overlap management should be treated as core
design concerns rather than late administrative cleanup.

### 3. The redesign should prepare students for mixed-state industry reality

The curriculum should not optimize for only one mode of software practice.

### 4. Governance language matters

A concept may be educationally strong but fail if it is framed in a way that is
hard to review, adopt, or adapt.

### 5. Early artifacts should guide, not pretend to finalize

At this stage, the goal is to establish a coherent path forward, not to claim
more certainty than the current evidence supports.

---

## Working Constraint Summary

The proposed AAISE effort is shaped by a combination of governance, timing,
transitional, curricular, and delivery constraints.

The program cannot be designed as if it were a blank-slate future degree with
no overlap, no approval timeline, and no continuity requirements.

At the same time, the redesign cannot remain trapped inside the logic of the
legacy program if that logic no longer matches the workforce reality students
are entering.

The design task is therefore bounded on both sides:

- by the realities of current institutional structure
- by the realities of emerging software practice

The work ahead must create coherence between those two realities rather than
pretend either one can be ignored.

---

## Next Questions

The next design-stage questions likely include:

- What graduate capabilities should remain stable across both the bridge and
  successor program models?
- Which current courses are most adaptable through AI injection, and which may
  point toward deeper future restructuring?
- What transition rules or equivalencies will eventually be needed to support
  student movement across overlap periods?
- How can the proposal be framed so that it is locally actionable and
  statewide legible at the same time?

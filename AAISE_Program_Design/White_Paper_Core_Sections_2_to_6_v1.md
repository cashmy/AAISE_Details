# White Paper Core Sections 2 to 6 v1

## 2. The Current Problem State

Wisconsin technical college software-related programs were largely designed for
a pre-AI development environment. That does not mean those programs no longer
have value. It does mean they were not originally structured for a workforce in
which AI now affects software work at multiple levels, including development
workflow, application behavior, internal business systems, and architectural
decision-making.

The current statewide conversation already reflects this recognition. The issue
is no longer whether change is needed. The more important question is what kind
of change is actually required.

The shallow version of the problem is that current software programs are missing
AI topics. The deeper version is that the programs were designed around an
earlier model of software practice, while students are now entering a field in
which multiple modes of practice coexist at the same time.

This means the redesign challenge is not simply to add content. It is to
prepare students for a mixed workforce reality while still working inside
present governance, advisory, and implementation constraints.

In that sense, the problem is both educational and operational.

Educationally, the curriculum must change because the field has changed.

Operationally, the change cannot be treated as if Wisconsin technical colleges
are starting from a blank slate. Existing programs remain in place. Current and
future program models may overlap. Students may move through more than one
curricular version during the transition. Faculty need an implementation path
that is realistic rather than purely aspirational.

The actual design problem, therefore, is not merely how to add AI to a software
program. It is how to create a responsible transition from legacy-approved
software curriculum to a more relevant AI-era model without breaking
employability, coherence, or institutional practicality.

## 3. Workforce Models for the AI Era

One reason software-program redesign can become confused is that the phrase
"AI in curriculum" is too broad. It can mean many different things, and if
those meanings are not separated, the conversation often collapses into narrow
tool-use assumptions.

For this reason, it is useful to distinguish among four Workforce Models that
now shape software work.

### Native

This is conventional software development without official AI participation in
either the product or the approved development workflow.

In some organizations, AI use may be culturally, managerially, or
operationally discouraged even if unofficial shadow use still occurs. This
remains a real workforce condition, which means students still need strong
conventional software capability.

### AI-Assisted

This is work in which AI supports the human workflow but is not part of the
delivered system itself.

This includes familiar low-barrier use cases such as drafting, summarization,
search, and research support. In software contexts, it also includes coding
assistance, debugging support, documentation drafting, comparison of options,
and other forms of development acceleration.

### AI-Injected

This is work in which AI is intentionally introduced into the application or
solution architecture as part of the system's designed behavior or supported
workflow.

This is not merely attaching a chatbot or adding a superficial AI feature. It
implies purposeful integration in which the role, boundaries, and value of AI
are architecturally justified.

This category can also include emerging agentic patterns in which AI
participates in multi-step operations, orchestrated tool use, or governed
action flows inside a designed system context.

### AI-Embedded

This is work in which AI becomes part of the internal operating logic of the
system or business workflow itself, especially in relation to organizational
data, analysis, prediction, transformation, decision support, or generative
operations.

Here AI is not peripheral. It participates directly in how the system functions
internally.

### Why These Distinctions Matter

These models matter because they explain why a modern software program cannot be
designed around only one future condition.

Graduates may enter:

- conventional software teams
- organizations using AI mainly as workflow assistance
- environments building AI-enabled applications
- environments using AI internally inside business processes and data systems

If curriculum redesign ignores that mixed reality, it will likely prepare
students either too narrowly or too abstractly.

## 4. What Has Changed in Software Practice

AI is not changing software work in only one way.

It is changing how software is written, how applications are conceived, how
teams work, how data is used, how architecture is evaluated, and how quickly
new abstractions move older manual work under the hood.

This matters because many existing curriculum assumptions were formed in an
environment where:

- programming work was more manually centered
- tool chains were less AI-mediated
- application logic was less influenced by probabilistic or model-based
  components
- data interaction was often taught as a narrower database topic rather than as
  part of a larger architectural system

That environment no longer describes the field well enough on its own.

At the same time, the field is not moving uniformly. Many businesses are still
adopting AI slowly. Some continue to operate in mostly conventional ways. Some
are using AI primarily as a productivity layer. Others are beginning to redesign
applications and internal systems around AI capabilities more directly.

This uneven adoption is precisely why software education must now prepare
students for coexistence across multiple Workforce Models rather than assuming a
single clean destination.

Another major change is pedagogical.

Traditional IT pedagogy has often assumed that strong preparation means exposing
students to many technologies and manual procedures in isolated form. In
practice, this can become a "teach everything" model that overloads the
curriculum while failing to build enough integrative capability. Many tasks that
once had to be taught as explicit manual procedure now sit under higher-level
abstractions, frameworks, commands, libraries, and AI-assisted workflows.

This does not remove the need for understanding. It changes where the
educational emphasis should sit.

Students still need conceptual grounding. They do not necessarily need every
historical manual procedure taught as if the field had not changed.

That shift has major implications for curriculum design.

## 5. Why Traditional “Add AI” Approaches Are Not Enough

Once the problem is framed correctly, it becomes clear why shallow modernization
strategies are unlikely to be sufficient.

The first common mistake is to treat AI as a later add-on topic. Under that
model, the existing software curriculum remains largely untouched and AI is
inserted through one or two advanced courses, a tool module, or a machine
learning elective. This approach may create awareness, but it does not
necessarily change the student's relationship to workflow, architecture, data,
systems reasoning, or professional judgment.

The second mistake is to reduce AI preparation to tool familiarity. Students may
be shown how to use chatbots, coding assistants, or prompt techniques, but they
may not be taught how to govern those tools, justify their use, validate their
outputs, or distinguish between AI as helper, AI as architectural component, and
AI as internal system logic.

The third mistake is inherited coverage inflation. A curriculum can become so
committed to broad isolated coverage that it continues teaching every layer of
manual detail even after the field has shifted toward higher-level abstractions,
framework support, and integrated development environments. This often creates
overloaded programs that feel comprehensive without actually becoming more
coherent.

A stronger approach is to redesign around capability value.

That means asking:

- what should graduates actually be able to do?
- what kinds of environments should they be able to work in?
- what conceptual understanding still matters even when implementation details
  are increasingly abstracted?
- what kinds of manual depth still need to be preserved, and where?

This is why simple AI insertion is not enough.

The challenge is not only to introduce new topics. It is to realign curriculum
structure, sequence, emphasis, and integration so that the program reflects the
field students are actually entering.

## 6. A Practical Transition Model: Bridge First, Successor Next

If the field has changed, and if shallow curriculum updates are insufficient,
the next question becomes how to redesign responsibly under current conditions.

The most practical answer is not immediate full replacement. It is a transition
model.

This paper proposes a two-track approach.

### Track 1. AI-Injected Bridge Revision of the Current Program

The first track uses the current approved IT Software Developer program as a
bridge structure for meaningful AI-injected modernization.

Its purpose is to:

- preserve the usable value of the existing approved program
- begin integrating AI-era capabilities now
- support students who still need a clear path through the current structure
- generate practical design evidence from real curriculum work

This track is not merely cosmetic renaming. It involves substantive reframing
of courses, earlier introduction of AI-assisted and AI-injected concepts, and
stronger integration of data, workflow, architecture, and justification of AI
use.

### Track 2. Future Associates AI Software Engineering (AAISE) Successor Program

The second track is the design of a more coherent future program rather than a
simple patching of the legacy structure.

Its purpose is to create a degree architecture intentionally aligned with the
mixed software reality described earlier in this paper.

This future Associates AI Software Engineering (AAISE) model would benefit from:

- cleaner sequencing
- more coherent course grouping
- stronger alignment to capability targets
- less dependence on legacy course-shell assumptions

### Why the Two Tracks Should Stay Connected

These tracks should not be treated as separate unrelated projects.

The bridge can inform the future program by revealing:

- what maps well into the current structure
- what becomes overloaded
- what should be preserved
- what likely needs a cleaner long-term design

The future successor model can also inform the bridge by clarifying which
capabilities and patterns matter enough to begin now, even before the final
program architecture exists.

### Why This Transition Model Matters

This approach addresses both educational and operational reality.

Educationally, it avoids pretending that minor AI insertion is enough.

Operationally, it avoids pretending that the final statewide solution can be
designed and implemented all at once without attention to overlap, transition,
and governance.

Most importantly, it creates a way to move forward now while still making room
for a more coherent future program later.

That is the central value of the bridge model: it allows modernization to begin
under current constraints while building toward a stronger AI-era program
architecture rather than remaining trapped inside legacy assumptions.

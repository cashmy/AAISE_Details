# AAISE Constraints and Design Realities v2

## Purpose

This document provides a revised view of the major constraints and design
realities shaping the proposed AAISE (Associates AI Software Engineering)
direction and its AI-injected bridge pathway.

It updates the earlier constraints artifact based on later curriculum and
bridge-design work.

It is not a complaint document.

Its purpose is to make the real boundaries, pressures, tradeoffs, and design
conditions visible early so that later program decisions remain coherent and
honest.

---

## Why a Revision Was Needed

The earlier constraints artifact established the major governance and transition
realities well.

Later bridge work, however, made several additional constraints much more
visible, especially around:

- sequencing
- course overload
- bridge versus successor divergence
- regional employability
- instructional evidence limits
- legacy "teach everything" coverage assumptions

These are now important enough to name directly.

---

## Constraint Category 1. Governance and Approval Reality

The current software-related programs exist within a statewide technical
college governance structure.

That means program-level change does not happen in isolation. Even when local
innovation is possible, it still exists within broader approval processes,
advisory expectations, and system-level coordination.

At present, proposed program changes must be submitted to the advisory
committee for review and approval by August 1. Once approved, changes would
then be implemented in the curriculum for the next academic year.

This creates a real design condition:

- ideas must be persuasive enough for review
- proposals must be structured enough to evaluate
- timing matters
- local experimentation must still be legible within statewide process

---

## Constraint Category 2. Transitional Rather Than Greenfield Design

This effort does not begin from a blank slate.

Southwest Tech is currently working within the approved IT Software Developer
program structure rather than launching an entirely unconstrained new program
immediately.

That means the task is not only how to invent a better future degree. It is
also how to work intelligently with what already exists.

This creates several implications:

- some existing program elements may need to be preserved temporarily
- some course descriptions may be changed only modestly
- content changes may be more flexible than structural changes
- near-term redesign may need to function as a bridge rather than an endpoint

This favors a staged design approach over a one-step replacement mindset.

---

## Constraint Category 3. Overlap Between Legacy and Future Programs

If a future AAISE program is approved, the current and future program
structures will likely overlap for at least one year.

This is not a minor administrative detail. It is a real design condition.

During that overlap period:

- some students may be completing the current program
- some may begin in a revised bridge version
- some may want or need to transition into the new structure
- advising, scheduling, and equivalency decisions will matter

Because of this, transition compatibility must be designed on purpose.

---

## Constraint Category 4. Uneven Current State of AI Injection

At present, Southwest Tech's AI-injection work is still unevenly distributed
across the full program.

The strongest redesign evidence appears in the first-semester bridge work,
especially in the Python course, with additional work underway or planned in
other early software courses.

This creates both a limitation and an opportunity.

The limitation is that the current work should not be overstated as a fully
validated end-state model.

The opportunity is that this work now provides real implementation patterns
that can inform later redesign more honestly than theory alone.

---

## Constraint Category 5. Evidence Without Full Validation

The bridge effort is no longer purely conceptual.

Some design patterns are already visible in actual instructional artifacts, such
as:

- staged AI integration
- differentiated AI roles
- bounded AI use
- earlier data and API exposure
- practical architectural framing

However, these patterns have not yet been validated through full instructional
delivery and student outcomes.

This creates an important reality condition:

the project now sits between theory and proof.

That position is valuable, but it must be described honestly.

---

## Constraint Category 6. Mixed-State Industry Reality

Industry is not moving in a single clean direction.

Some employers remain largely conventional in their software practices. Others
are adopting AI primarily as a productivity tool. Others are beginning to
inject AI into applications and workflows. Still others are embedding AI into
business systems more directly.

Because of this, the target environment for graduates is mixed rather than
uniform.

The curriculum therefore cannot optimize only for a fully AI-native future
while ignoring the continued reality of conventional and partially transitioned
environments.

At the same time, it cannot remain anchored only to conventional practice and
assume AI capability can be added later as a minor layer.

---

## Constraint Category 7. Regional Employability Reality

The bridge cannot be designed only around conceptual elegance or future-state
AI identity.

It also has to preserve regionally credible employability pathways.

This became more concrete during discussion of courses such as C#/.NET, where
regional workforce relevance may justify retaining and reframing a course even
if it is not the most theoretically streamlined option.

This means the design must balance:

- future readiness
- local employability
- conceptual coherence

rather than assuming those always align automatically.

---

## Constraint Category 8. Legacy Coverage Assumption

Traditional IT pedagogy often assumes that stronger preparation means exposing
students to a broad range of technologies and manual procedures in isolated
form.

This often creates a "teach everything" or "kitchen sink" pattern in which:

- technologies are taught separately rather than integratively
- manual tasks are emphasized even when modern abstractions now handle them
- older implementation expectations persist after the field has changed
- curricular breadth expands without equivalent gains in real capability

Many technologies increasingly move once-manual work under the hood through:

- higher-level abstractions
- richer libraries and frameworks
- new syntax and commands
- layered tooling
- AI-assisted workflow support

Examples include things such as context managers replacing more manual resource
handling patterns, built-in collection operations replacing older stepwise
procedures, and framework conventions reducing the need to teach every lower
level task in raw form.

This does not mean students no longer need conceptual understanding.

It means curriculum should distinguish more carefully between:

- what students must understand conceptually
- what students must still perform manually
- what can now be approached through higher-order architectural or workflow
  understanding

This constraint matters because overload is not solved only by cutting content.

It is also solved by teaching the right conceptual layer.

---

## Constraint Category 9. Overload and Course-Carrying Capacity

Even when the bridge direction is strong, individual courses cannot carry
unlimited redesign weight.

Several current courses are at risk of overload if reframed without discipline.

Examples include:

- C# if ASP.NET exposure is merged in too heavily
- Advanced Python if it absorbs too much ML/AI, notebooks, libraries, and web
  framework work without clear boundaries
- Agile Practices if it becomes agile, GitHub, pull requests, review, CI/CD,
  and governance all at once

This means bridge design is constrained not only by what belongs in the
curriculum, but also by what one course can realistically carry well.

---

## Constraint Category 10. Sequencing Constraint

The bridge may not always be able to use the cleanest pedagogical order.

A good example is the SQL and data-modeling sequence.

The cleaner long-term AAISE logic may be:

- modern data modeling first
- SQL implementation second

But the bridge may need to preserve a practical order in which:

- SQL remains earlier
- broader modern data modeling appears later in the slot replacing Advanced SQL

This is a real design constraint, not just a minor scheduling detail.

It means the bridge and the successor program may sometimes differ not because
the bridge logic is wrong, but because the bridge is absorbing structural
constraints the successor may later be able to remove.

---

## Constraint Category 11. Course-Slot Identity Constraint

Some inherited course slots are only useful if their educational identity is
redefined.

For example, a course slot such as C++ may be viable not as generic additional
language coverage, but as a systems-performance and abstraction-awareness
course.

This means the bridge cannot simply ask:

"keep or remove the course?"

It must often ask:

"what should this course slot now be for?"

That is a more architectural question than traditional curriculum mapping often
assumes.

---

## Constraint Category 12. Bridge Versus Successor Divergence

Some elements should likely remain stable across both the bridge and the future
AAISE successor.

Others will likely diverge.

For example:

- foundational Python work likely persists across both
- AI-assisted workflow capability likely persists across both
- systems analysis and architectural thinking likely persist across both
- sequencing, course grouping, and depth may differ between bridge and
  successor forms

This means the design process must actively distinguish between:

- bridge-specific compromises
- successor-specific architecture
- shared enduring program logic

Without this distinction, the bridge may either become too conservative or try
to become the full successor too early.

---

## Constraint Category 13. Communication-Layer Constraint

The work now exists at multiple communication layers.

Different audiences require different forms of the same underlying logic.

For example:

- deep design artifacts for internal architectural thinking
- condensed matrices for supervisor and management review
- white-paper framing for advisory and statewide discussion

This means communication format is not merely presentation.

It is part of the design reality.

If the design cannot be translated across these layers, it becomes harder to
adopt, review, and advocate for.

---

## Constraint Category 14. Replacement-Slot Pressure

Once weak-fit or legacy-heavy courses are identified for removal or redesign,
the newly available slots become high-stakes decisions.

These openings can improve coherence significantly, but they can also create
pressure to overfill the curriculum with novelty or ambition.

This means replacement decisions should be governed by clear educational job
definitions, not by the excitement of having open space.

---

## Design Implications

Taken together, these constraints suggest several design implications.

### 1. The bridge should be governed by capability value, not inherited coverage habits

Curriculum breadth should be justified by what students need to be able to do,
not by legacy assumptions that every manual or isolated technology task must be
covered separately.

### 2. The bridge and successor program should not be forced into artificial sameness

Some bridge decisions will be compromise decisions. That is acceptable as long
as the compromise is named honestly.

### 3. Strong ideas still need delivery discipline

A good concept can fail if it overloads courses, outruns faculty capacity, or
becomes too hard to explain to decision-makers.

### 4. Instructional evidence should be used, but not overstated

Current bridge artifacts are valuable design evidence, but they are not yet
full proof of instructional outcomes.

### 5. Replacement slots should be treated as strategic assets

Freed curricular space should be used to strengthen coherence, not merely to
add more content.

---

## Working Constraint Summary

The AAISE and bridge design effort is constrained not only by governance and
timing, but also by pedagogy, sequencing, regional employability, course
carrying capacity, and communication format.

The resulting task is therefore not simply to add AI content to an existing
program.

It is to redesign a curriculum architecture in a way that:

- works under present institutional realities
- reflects modern software practice more honestly
- avoids inherited coverage overload
- preserves employability
- creates a transition path toward a stronger future program

# 3. Opening Frame (2-4 minutes)

Today is the checkpoint where project planning becomes approval-ready or revision-ready.

Key message:

* the capstone must be realistic
* approval is about fit, not enthusiasm alone
* a clear smaller project is stronger than an oversized weak one

Suggested wording:

> "Approval is not about whether the idea sounds exciting. Approval is about whether the project fits the course, the timeline, and your ability to explain what you are building."

---

# 4. Course Positioning (2-4 minutes)

Students now know:

* how to frame a project
* how to reduce scope
* how to name structure and risk

Today adds:

* proposal quality checkpoint
* project path comparison
* approval criteria

Suggested wording:

> "This session turns your project planning into a reviewable capstone proposal. The focus is not adding more ideas. The focus is making the plan clear enough to approve or revise intelligently."

---

# 5. Core Concepts (10-20 minutes)

## Concept 1 - A capstone must fit the course

Plain-language explanation:

* the project should use course concepts without exceeding the course scope

Why it matters:

* approval should protect students from building something they cannot finish or explain

## Concept 2 - Project path matters

Plain-language explanation:

* console, data-driven, API-based, or light architecture-preview paths do not all carry the same complexity

Why it matters:

* students should understand what kind of project they are actually proposing

## Concept 3 - AI boundaries should be explicit before the build begins

Plain-language explanation:

* students should decide what AI may help with and what remains their responsibility

Why it matters:

* accountability becomes easier if boundaries are set before pressure increases during the build

## Concept 4 - Approval can include revision

Plain-language explanation:

* revision is not rejection in a personal sense
* revision is a project-quality improvement step

Why it matters:

* students should not interpret capstone revision as failure

# 7. Hands-On / Lab Bridge (10-20 minutes)

Students should:

* submit the capstone proposal
* justify project type
* justify structure
* justify AI-use boundaries
* prepare for either approval or targeted revision

Suggested wording:

> "Your goal today is not to defend every original idea. Your goal is to leave with a project plan that is realistic enough to build and explain well."

Do not require yet:

* major new coding
* feature expansion
* polished build artifacts

---

# 8. Common Mistakes / Watch-Fors (5-8 minutes)

## Mistake 1 - students argue for ambition instead of fit

Why it happens:

* more features can feel like a stronger project

Correction:

* keep returning to explainability, finishability, and course fit

## Mistake 2 - AI-use plan is generic

Why it happens:

* students may write broad statements like "AI will help with coding"

Correction:

* require a more specific boundary and accountability statement

## Mistake 3 - students think revision means the project idea failed

Why it happens:

* proposal revision can feel like personal rejection

Correction:

* frame revision as a professional quality checkpoint

---

# 9. Explain / Checkpoint Questions (3-8 minutes)

Ask:

* Why does this project fit the course?
* What makes the scope realistic?
* What structure are you expecting to use?
* What should AI not decide for you during the build?
* What revision would improve this proposal if needed?

---

# 10. End-of-Class Success Check

> By the end of this session, students should be able to submit or revise a capstone proposal that clearly states project type, scope, structure, risks, and AI-use boundaries in a way that supports approval.

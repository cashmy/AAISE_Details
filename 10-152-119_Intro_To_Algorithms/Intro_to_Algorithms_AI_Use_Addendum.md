# Course Position

This course is an algorithmic judgment course.

Students are not only learning how to make code run. They are learning how to:

- frame algorithmic problems
- identify inputs, outputs, assumptions, and constraints
- choose appropriate data structures and solution strategies
- test correctness with evidence
- compare approaches for readability, efficiency, and fit
- explain algorithm behavior clearly
- critique AI-assisted solutions instead of accepting them blindly

AI-assisted tools may support this work, but they may not replace the student's
responsibility to reason, test, compare, and explain.

---

# Central AI Principle for This Course

AI may help produce or improve an algorithmic solution.

AI may not replace the student's algorithmic understanding.

In this course, responsible AI use means:

```text
frame first -> attempt or trace -> use AI within the boundary ->
test with evidence -> compare and explain -> revise responsibly
```

If a student cannot explain the assumptions, evidence, tradeoffs, or behavior of
a solution, the student does not yet own the solution.

---

# AI Progression in This Course

This course follows the broader program AI involvement model:

```text
Manual First -> AI-Assisted -> AI-Injected -> AI-Integrated
```

## Manual First

Students begin by framing the problem and attempting reasoning before relying
on AI.

Manual-first work may include:

- identifying inputs and outputs
- listing assumptions and constraints
- writing pseudocode
- tracing small examples by hand
- predicting behavior before running code
- creating initial tests or edge cases

Manual-first does not require perfect code. It requires the student to begin
with their own reasoning.

## AI-Assisted

AI may support learning, explanation, critique, and comparison after the student
has established an initial frame.

Appropriate AI-assisted uses include:

- explaining unfamiliar algorithm vocabulary
- comparing two approaches conceptually
- suggesting edge cases to test
- helping interpret timing or trace evidence
- critiquing a student-written approach
- explaining why a data structure might fit or fail

AI-assisted use should strengthen student reasoning rather than replace it.

## AI-Injected

AI may generate or revise code only when the assignment allows it and the
student has enough structure to evaluate the result.

Appropriate AI-injected uses include:

- generating a candidate implementation after manual framing
- revising code for readability
- helping repair a bug after the student attempts diagnosis
- suggesting an alternate implementation for comparison

When AI-injected work is allowed, students must test, justify, and explain the
result.

## AI-Integrated

AI-integrated work is optional or preview-level in this course.

It may appear only when a task has enough structure for students to preserve
authorship and accountability. The course does not require AI to become a full
collaborative development partner in every assignment.

---

# AI Restrictions in This Course

Students may not:

- use AI to bypass required manual-first framing, tracing, or testing
- submit AI-generated code or explanations they cannot defend
- treat AI-generated algorithms as automatically correct
- use AI output without checking assumptions and edge cases
- replace required evidence with AI's claim that the solution works
- hide AI use when disclosure is required
- use AI to avoid explaining complexity, tradeoffs, or correctness

Unexamined AI output is not acceptable algorithmic work.

---

# Evidence Expectations

Because this is an algorithms course, AI accountability must be tied to
evidence.

Students may be asked to provide:

- trace tables
- timing tables
- growth comparisons
- test cases
- edge-case results
- comparison tables
- graph diagrams or traversal orders
- similarity, ranking, clustering, or hashing evidence
- explanation of assumptions and limitations

AI may help suggest evidence, but it may not replace the student's responsibility
to produce and interpret evidence.

---

# Disclosure Expectations

When AI use is permitted or required to be disclosed, students should be
prepared to explain:

- what problem they were solving
- what they attempted or reasoned through first
- what AI was used for
- what AI produced or suggested
- what they accepted, changed, or rejected
- how they tested or validated the result
- what assumptions or edge cases matter
- what tradeoffs remain
- what decisions remained theirs

This disclosure may occur through a lab reflection, final submission note,
personalized explanation defense, or instructor questioning.

---

# Relationship to the Final Assessment

The final assessment includes:

```text
Part 1 - Applied Solution Set
Part 2 - Personalized Explanation Defense
```

AI may be allowed in Part 1 when the instructor permits it.

If AI is used, students must still submit evidence and be ready to explain their
own work in Part 2.

Part 2 is not a gotcha assessment. Its purpose is to verify that students
understand the solutions they submitted, including assumptions, tests,
tradeoffs, limitations, and any AI contribution.

---

# Conceptual Understanding and Logic Recall

This course values conceptual understanding over memorized reproduction of exact
logic patterns.

Students should know enough Python and algorithmic structure to read, trace,
test, and explain solutions. However, the primary goal is not blind recall of
every algorithm from memory.

The primary goal is that students can:

- recognize the kind of problem being solved
- choose or critique a reasonable approach
- identify relevant data structures
- test whether the solution works
- compare tradeoffs
- explain the behavior and limits of the solution
- use AI support without surrendering judgment

AI can often help correct implementation details when the concept is sound.

AI cannot reliably rescue a student who does not understand what problem is
being solved or why an approach fits.

---

# Final Standard for This Course

In `10-152-119 Algorithmic Problem Solving`, responsible AI use means:

- the student begins with problem framing and reasoning
- AI support is used within the assignment boundary
- generated or revised work is tested with evidence
- the student can explain correctness, assumptions, tradeoffs, and limits
- the student remains responsible for the final solution

The central question is not:

> Did AI help produce the solution?

The central question is:

> Can the student evaluate, adapt, and explain the algorithmic approach
> responsibly?

## Heuristic Comparison

| Work Mode | Likely Strength | Likely Bottleneck | Heuristic Time Pattern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Native solo design, no AI | Deep local ownership and coherent instructor voice | Cognitive load, drafting volume, cross-artifact consistency, fatigue | Slowest for artifact volume; highest mental burden |
| Team-based native design | Multiple perspectives and review | Meetings, coordination, buy-in, version reconciliation, delayed decisions | Often slower than solo for early artifact creation |
| AI-assisted design with strong human direction | High throughput, fast iteration, rapid cross-artifact alignment | Requires strong human judgment, review, scope control, and correction | Compresses drafting, refactoring, and alignment loops dramatically |

## Native Solo Work Estimate

A non-AI-assisted solo instructor/designer would likely need to perform all of
the following manually:

- hold the full course sequence in working memory
- interpret the course description and WIDS needs
- map prerequisites and downstream course needs
- read and curate textbook coverage
- create assignment progression
- draft each assignment
- draft rubrics
- draft final assessment logic
- create lecture outlines
- check alignment across all artifacts
- refactor naming, folders, and references
- preserve design rationale for later use

Heuristic estimate:

```text
For one course at this level of detail:
40-80+ hours of concentrated solo design work

For multiple courses across a sequence:
several weeks to months, depending on prior material and interruption load
```

The main limiting factor is not typing speed. It is sustained design cognition:
holding relationships, constraints, pedagogy, assessment logic, and future
implementation consequences together at once.

## Native Team Work Estimate

A team environment can improve critique and legitimacy, but early artifact
production often slows because of coordination overhead.

Typical added costs:

- meeting time
- explanation time
- waiting between reviews
- negotiation of scope and terminology
- resolving competing pedagogical instincts
- reconciling document versions
- ensuring one artifact does not contradict another

Heuristic estimate:

```text
For one course at this level of detail:
2-6 calendar weeks is plausible, even if total person-hours vary

For a 7-course design set:
one academic term or longer is plausible if done through normal committee flow
```

This is not because teams are ineffective. It is because curriculum artifacts
require shared mental models before they can move quickly.

## AI-Assisted Design Pattern Observed

The observed AI-assisted pattern compressed several normally separate phases:

```text
framing -> drafting -> critique -> refactor -> alignment -> documentation
```

into a continuous loop.

The human role shifted from primary drafter to:

- architect
- reviewer
- scope governor
- pedagogy holder
- alignment judge
- institutional constraint interpreter
- final decision-maker

The AI role functioned as:

- drafting accelerator
- structure generator
- consistency checker
- refactoring assistant
- memory extender
- alternative-path simulator
- artifact integration partner

## Why the Output Was Viable

The output was viable because the work was not treated as raw generation.

Quality came from:

- clear course descriptions
- explicit constraints
- prior artifact patterns
- iterative correction
- folder and naming discipline
- WIDS alignment
- repeated sanity checks
- human decisions about pedagogy and workload
- willingness to refactor when the design changed

The AI did not replace instructional judgment. It amplified the amount of
judgment that could be expressed in durable artifacts.

## Throughput Difference

A rough qualitative map:

| Activity | Native Solo | Native Team | AI-Assisted With Human Governance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial artifact drafting | Slow and fatiguing | Slow to moderate | Fast |
| Cross-artifact alignment | High cognitive burden | Often delayed until review | Continuous and iterative |
| Refactoring after design changes | Painful and easy to miss references | Requires coordination | Fast if source intent is clear |
| Pedagogical consistency | Depends on one person's stamina | Requires shared model | Strong when human keeps governing frame |
| Institutional readability | Requires careful after-pass | Often reviewed late | Can be built into artifacts early |
| Documentation of rationale | Often skipped under time pressure | Often scattered in meeting notes | Can be captured immediately |

## The Most Important Difference

The largest gain is not simply speed.

The largest gain is compression of the design-feedback loop.

In native work, these often happen separately:

```text
think -> draft -> pause -> review -> revise -> align -> document
```

In the observed AI-assisted workflow, they happened as a single sustained design
conversation:

```text
think + draft + review + refactor + align + preserve rationale
```

That matters because curriculum design loses quality when rationale is separated
from artifact production.

## Risks and Guardrails

AI-assisted curriculum design can create poor output quickly if the process is
not governed.

Risks:

- plausible but misaligned artifacts
- excessive generic language
- hidden workload inflation
- loss of instructor voice
- overproduction of documents without implementation value
- weak assessment alignment
- false confidence in untested structures

Guardrails used in this work:

- compare against existing course patterns
- name instructional intent before writing artifacts
- keep WIDS/state requirements visible
- preserve folder scaffolding and artifact maps
- refactor after major design decisions
- create decision logs and risk logs
- defer details until coding or decks reveal actual needs

## Reflection

The native thought process alone for this work is enormous.

The hardest part is not writing the documents. The hardest part is maintaining
the relationships among:

- course outcomes
- WIDS requirements
- student readiness
- labs
- demos
- final assessment
- rubrics
- AI policy
- downstream course preparation
- instructor workload
- future refactor risk

AI-assisted design made it possible to externalize and stabilize those
relationships quickly enough that they could be reviewed, corrected, and turned
into usable artifacts within the same work session.

That is the meaningful educational-design velocity gain.

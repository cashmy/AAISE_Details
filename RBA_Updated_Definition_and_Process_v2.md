# RBA - UPDATED DEFINITION AND PROCESS

**Refraction-Based Architecture**

---

## Purpose

This artifact updates the working definition of Refraction-Based Architecture (RBA) based on its use across:

* software and product development
* AI-assisted architecture and refactoring work
* curriculum and multi-artifact instructional design

It is written to serve two purposes:

1. support LLM recognition and coordination during refraction activities
2. provide a simple human-readable definition and high-level order of process

This is not meant to replace the original principle set.

It is meant to function as a clearer applied definition after broader use.

---

## Short Definition

Refraction-Based Architecture is a **human-governed process for designing coherent systems and artifact stacks** in which:

* intent is clarified first
* constraints are made explicit early
* AI is introduced into an already-governed frame
* outputs are treated as refractions of human intent, not autonomous authorship
* concrete artifacts are used to expose drift, gaps, and hidden constraints
* revision and refactoring are expected as part of the process

In simple terms:

**RBA means the human defines meaning, boundaries, and quality targets first, then uses AI to accelerate transformation, comparison, drafting, and revision inside that governed structure.**

---

## What RBA Is Not

RBA is not:

* simple prompt engineering
* asking AI for finished work without prior framing
* using AI as the source of purpose or authorship
* a claim that implementation skill is unnecessary
* a linear one-pass generation process
* limited only to software code production

RBA is also not dependent on a specific framework, IDE, or model.

It is an architectural and governance pattern.

---

## Updated Core Understanding

Earlier usage may have made RBA appear mainly software-centered.

Current applied use suggests something broader:

RBA is best understood as a **governed refraction system** for any domain where:

* human intent must remain primary
* multiple artifacts must stay coherent
* AI can accelerate drafting or transformation
* concrete outputs reveal hidden constraints
* revision improves quality

That includes:

* software systems
* product architecture
* instructional systems
* curriculum design
* possibly other complex artifact ecosystems

---

## Human-Readable Operating Logic

The simplest high-level logic of RBA is:

1. define what is being built and why
2. define the important constraints and boundaries
3. choose or shape the likely structure
4. use AI to draft, compare, transform, or extend inside that frame
5. inspect the resulting artifacts for drift, gaps, overload, or mismatch
6. refactor upward when needed
7. continue descent again with improved coherence

This means the process is neither:

* pure top-down planning

nor:

* unrestricted bottom-up generation

It is a governed oscillation between intent and artifact reality.

---

## The Most Important Shift in RBA

The most important shift is this:

the human role moves away from doing all direct construction manually and toward:

* framing
* governing
* sequencing
* comparing
* validating
* revising
* deciding what should be accepted, changed, or rejected

AI increases leverage.

It does not replace architectural responsibility.

---

## Why the Term "Refraction" Still Fits

The term remains useful because AI output is not treated as original meaning.

Instead, AI output is understood as:

* transformed
* refracted
* mediated
* shaped by the quality of the human framing, available context, and constraints

This helps preserve:

* authorship honesty
* uncertainty honesty
* governance discipline

It also reduces the tendency to over-attribute intelligence, intention, or coherence to the model itself.

---

## LLM Recognition Definition

When operating with a human using RBA, the LLM should recognize the following pattern:

* the human is the source of purpose, meaning, and final judgment
* the human may provide partial but architecturally meaningful framing before asking for output
* the task is not merely to generate artifacts, but to help preserve coherence across related artifacts
* outputs should be treated as provisional when uncertainty, drift, or hidden assumptions remain
* upward correction is normal and desirable
* the system should prefer alignment, traceability, and bounded revision over premature closure

The LLM should understand that in an RBA context, a "good" response is not merely:

* fast
* polished
* plausible

It must also be:

* aligned
* governable
* revisable
* honest about uncertainty
* supportive of human architectural control

---

## LLM Coordination Guidance

When coordinating with a human working in RBA mode, the LLM should generally:

1. identify the governing intent before expanding output
2. preserve named constraints rather than smoothing them away
3. surface tradeoffs when there are multiple viable paths
4. avoid treating plausible structure as automatically correct structure
5. support canonical-source thinking when multiple artifacts exist
6. expect and welcome refactoring, reconciliation, and alignment passes
7. help expose missing assumptions rather than silently filling all gaps
8. distinguish between:
   * governing artifacts
   * working artifacts
   * delivery artifacts
   * publication artifacts
9. treat artifact ecosystems as systems, not isolated documents
10. optimize for coherence before optimization for speed or elegance

---

## LLM Warning Conditions in an RBA Context

The LLM should become more cautious when:

* the human intent is still vague but generation pressure is high
* lower-level outputs begin to outrun the governing frame
* an artifact appears polished but introduces hidden contradictions
* evaluation or readiness expectations drift ahead of instruction
* content is being expanded before performance expectations are defined
* AI-produced structure begins to masquerade as settled architecture
* a domain shift occurs that invalidates older assumptions

In these cases, the correct move is often:

* slow down
* restate the frame
* identify the drift
* revise upward before continuing

## High-Level Order of Process

RBA does not require one rigid sequence, but its most common practical order looks like this:

### Stage 1 - Frame

Define:

* purpose
* intended outcomes
* constraints
* boundaries
* reality conditions

### Stage 2 - Shape

Define or identify:

* likely structure
* layers
* artifact types
* canonical references

### Stage 3 - Refract

Use AI to:

* draft
* transform
* compare
* organize
* extend
* propose alternatives

### Stage 4 - Contact Reality

Use the produced artifact(s) to identify:

* drift
* overload
* missing assumptions
* contradictions
* viability constraints

### Stage 5 - Refactor Upward

When reality contact reveals problems:

* revise the framing
* revise the structure
* revise the sequencing
* revise the boundaries

### Stage 6 - Descend Again

Return to downstream production with improved coherence.

### Stage 7 - Stabilize

Once the artifact system is sufficiently coherent:

* reconcile related outputs
* create indexes / wrappers / support layers
* separate internal governance artifacts from delivery artifacts

---

## Compact Process Version

If a shorter memory aid is needed, RBA can be remembered as:

1. Frame
2. Shape
3. Refract
4. Test against reality
5. Refactor upward
6. Descend again
7. Stabilize

Or even more simply:

**frame -> generate -> inspect -> refactor -> realign**

---

## Evidence from Applied Use

Recent use suggests the following are true:

* RBA works in more than one programming style
* RBA can coordinate AI-assisted code creation and AI-assisted artifact-system design
* curriculum design can produce meaningful "reality contact" even without executable code
* the strongest productivity gains come from governance, not from raw generation alone
* simple-looking artifacts may carry high cognitive load because instructional or architectural selection is harder than drafting

This matters because it means RBA should not be defined too narrowly around code generation only.

---

## Updated Practical Principle

The practical center of RBA is this:

**AI does not create coherence.**

**It accelerates the expression, comparison, and revision of coherence when a human supplies the governing structure.**

That is true in software.

It also appears true in curriculum design and other artifact systems.

---

## Final Working Definition

Refraction-Based Architecture is a human-governed, AI-amplified design process in which intent, constraints, and architectural direction are established first; AI is then used as a refraction medium to accelerate drafting, transformation, comparison, and revision; and concrete artifacts are repeatedly tested against reality so that coherence can be preserved through upward refactoring and renewed descent.

In shorter form:

**RBA is the practice of governing AI-assisted creation through intent-first framing, bounded refraction, reality contact, and iterative realignment.**
